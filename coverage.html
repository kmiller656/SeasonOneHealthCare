What's actually in Season One

A straight account of what is in the app: the question bank, the lessons, the reference library, and where each of them is still thin.

1,900 questions 950 conditions 950 lessons Ordered by exam yield

Coverage, as of today

Season One is organized the way the exam is: by condition, and each condition is placed against the PAEA/NCCPA blueprint's rotations and task areas. That's the same structure that produces the seven end-of-rotation (EOR) exams and the PANCE itself. Conditions were written in blueprint-yield order, not alphabetically, so the highest-tested material exists first.

Today that coverage is deep across six rotations: Family Medicine, Emergency Medicine, Internal Medicine, Pediatrics, Women's Health, and Psychiatry. It's just getting started in Surgery. We'd rather say that plainly than round it up.

Lessons

Every condition has a lesson: a short walk-through you read start to finish, rather than a page you look something up on. Each one moves through the same fixed sequence, so the format never has to be relearned: what it is, the symptoms, how it is diagnosed, the treatment plan with its lines named, and a closing High Yield PANCE page listing what gets tested and the wording a question uses when it tests it. A short exam ends the lesson, drawn from the bank and from the conditions the lesson told you to tell it apart from.

Depth is uneven and we will say so. Dermatology, hematology and endocrine are all but finished: mechanism, risk factors, laboratory findings, published criteria where they exist, and a lined treatment plan. That is 500 of the 950 today, and no blueprint task area is left at zero. The thinnest are musculoskeletal, EENOT and renal/genitourinary. The remaining lessons are shorter, and are being rewritten section by section. The blueprint page shows exactly which task areas are done and which are not.

The reference library

Alongside the lessons there is a condition reference covering all 950 terms, and a medication reference organized by class. Where a condition's lesson has been written at full depth, its reference page is built from that lesson, so the two never drift into telling you different things about the same disease.

Flashcards

You can build your own decks in the app, with text and images on either side, and review them on a spaced repetition schedule. Decks you already have elsewhere can be brought in as a plain text file (.txt, .csv or .tsv), and any deck can be exported the same way and handed to someone else. Nothing is locked in.

How a condition is tested

Every condition carries two questions, each testing something different. One asks you to recognize it: the same first move a clinician makes. The other assumes you already know what it is and tests what you'd actually do about it, managing it. A third layer isn't in the bundled bank yet (complications, mechanisms, the kind of twist that changes the usual answer), and we're not implying it is.

The whole bank, not a rationed slice

Board prep in this category is normally handed out one rationed slice at a time, with the rest held back for later. Season One's approach is the opposite: build the whole thing first, and ship the entire 1,900-question core, all 950 lessons and both reference libraries together. It works entirely offline, on a device you already own, and your progress follows you between your own devices through iCloud. It is still in beta and is being built in the open.

How this content is made

The question bank is AI-drafted, written to the PAEA/NCCPA blueprint, and going through a review pass. The lessons are hand-written. Neither is clinician-verified, and we won't claim otherwise. Automated checks confirm every question is answerable from the app's own study content and validate drug names against the National Library of Medicine's RxNorm database. Full detail is in Privacy & Terms.

Who this is for

Season One is built around you studying at 11pm the night before a rotation exam, not around a curriculum committee. The questions, the lessons and the offline access are decided from that one seat: a student who needs the next thing they read to be worth their time, tonight.
